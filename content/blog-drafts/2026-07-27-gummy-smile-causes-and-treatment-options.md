A gummy smile, meaning that you show more gum than you would like when you smile, has at least four different causes, and they need four different treatments. The gums may be covering more of the teeth than they should, the teeth themselves may be short or worn, the upper lip may rise unusually high, or the upper jaw may sit lower than average. **Anyone who recommends a treatment before working out which of these applies to you is guessing.** The good news is that most causes have a well-established option, and the assessment that tells them apart is straightforward.

## How much gum showing is normal?

There is no strict rule, and fashion is not a diagnosis. As a rough guide, dentists consider up to about two millimetres of gum on a full smile to be within the usual range, and around three to four millimetres or more is when people typically start noticing it. What matters far more than the number is whether it bothers *you*, and whether the gum tissue is healthy.

## What causes a gummy smile?

- **Altered passive eruption.** The teeth erupted normally but the gum never receded to its final position, so it still covers part of the crown. The teeth look short and square, though the full-length tooth is there underneath.
- **Gum overgrowth, or gingival enlargement.** Swollen, enlarged gum tissue caused by plaque and inflammation, sometimes by braces, and sometimes by certain medications for blood pressure, epilepsy or immune suppression.
- **A short or highly mobile upper lip.** The lip itself is short, or the muscles lift it further than average when you smile, exposing more gum.
- **Vertical maxillary excess.** The upper jaw has grown longer vertically, so the whole dental arch sits lower in the face. This is skeletal rather than gum-related.
- **Worn or short teeth.** Grinding, erosion or wear has shortened the visible teeth over the years, so the gum-to-tooth ratio shifts.
- **Some combination of the above**, which is common.

## How does the dentist work out which one it is?

Expect a short, methodical assessment rather than a glance:

- Measuring how much gum shows at rest, on a normal smile and on a full smile
- Measuring the proportions of the front teeth, because a central incisor that measures short is a different problem from one that is normal length but partly covered
- Watching how far your upper lip travels when you smile, and how long the lip is at rest
- Probing the gum to see where the bone level sits relative to the tooth
- **X-rays**, and for suspected jaw causes, a facial or cephalometric assessment
- Checking for gum inflammation, plaque levels and any medication that could be enlarging the tissue

## What are the treatment options for each cause?

- **Inflammation or plaque-related overgrowth.** Start with professional cleaning and a better home routine. Gum tissue often shrinks back significantly once inflammation resolves, and this step costs the least and risks nothing. It also has to happen before any surgical option anyway.
- **Medication-related overgrowth.** Your dentist may liaise with your doctor about whether the medication can be changed. Never stop a prescribed medication on your own.
- **Altered passive eruption.** Crown lengthening, a gum contouring procedure that reshapes the gum line, sometimes with a small amount of bone recontouring, to expose the full natural length of the teeth.
- **Short or worn teeth.** Composite bonding, veneers or crowns to restore the height that has been lost, often combined with treating the grinding that caused it.
- **Highly mobile upper lip.** Lip repositioning surgery is the lasting option. Temporary muscle-relaxant injections are used in some clinics to limit lip lift, but the effect wears off after a few months and needs repeating.
- **Teeth positioned too low.** Braces or clear aligners can intrude the front teeth upwards, sometimes with temporary anchorage devices, changing the smile line without surgery.
- **Vertical maxillary excess.** The definitive treatment is orthognathic, or jaw, surgery, usually combined with orthodontics and planned jointly with a maxillofacial surgeon. This is a significant undertaking and only appropriate for genuinely skeletal cases.

Not every option is right for every patient, and some cases need a referral or a combined plan across more than one discipline. An honest assessment includes being told which route is not suitable for you and why.

## Can unusually long front teeth be shortened?

Often, yes, and it is the mirror image of the same question about proportion. Depending on the cause:

- **Enamel contouring or reshaping.** Small amounts of enamel are smoothed to shorten or even up the edges. It is quick, usually painless, and needs no anaesthetic. It is also irreversible, because enamel does not grow back, so a conservative dentist removes as little as possible.
- **Composite bonding.** Reshaping by adding material rather than removing it, useful when teeth are uneven rather than uniformly long.
- **Crowns or veneers.** For teeth that also need strengthening or a full change of shape.
- **Orthodontics.** When the teeth are not actually long but sit lower than the neighbouring teeth, moving them is the correct fix rather than grinding them down.

Teeth that look long because the gum has receded are a different problem again. The answer there is treating the gum recession and its cause rather than trimming the tooth.

## How many visits does it take?

- Gum contouring or crown lengthening: usually one treatment visit plus a review, with gum tissue settling over several weeks to a few months before any final cosmetic work
- Bonding: often one visit
- Veneers or crowns: typically two to three visits
- Orthodontic intrusion: months, as part of a full treatment plan
- Jaw surgery: a staged plan over a year or more, including orthodontics before and after

If gum contouring and veneers are both planned, the gums must be allowed to heal and stabilise before the final restorations are made. Rushing that step is how gum lines end up uneven a year later.

## Who is not a good candidate?

Active gum disease must be treated first. Uncontrolled diabetes, heavy smoking and poor plaque control all affect healing after any gum procedure. If the underlying cause is skeletal, gum contouring alone will not fix it, and having it done anyway is how people end up disappointed after spending money. Cosmetic gum treatment during pregnancy is usually deferred, though gum inflammation in pregnancy should always be treated.
