Tight contacts between teeth are normal anatomy. Plenty of people have them, and they are not a fault. But there is one distinction worth learning, because it changes what you should do next. **Floss that is hard to push through everywhere is usually a technique or product problem. Floss that catches, frays or shreds in the same one spot every time is usually a physical problem in that gap, and it needs a dentist to look at it.** Shredding floss is one of the most reliable early warnings patients can spot themselves.

## Why does floss shred or get stuck in one particular spot?

Floss frays when it is dragged across something rough. In a single repeating location, the usual culprits are:

- **An overhanging or rough filling margin**, where the edge of a filling sits slightly proud of the tooth
- **Decay starting between the two teeth**, where the surface has broken and left a sharp rim
- **A chipped or fractured edge** of enamel
- **Calculus, or hardened tartar**, built up below the contact point
- **The margin of a crown or bridge** that has worn or was never perfectly smooth

None of those are fixed by trying harder. They are also all easier to treat early. An overhang can be smoothed in one short appointment, whereas the decay that has been quietly collecting plaque against it for a year is a filling or worse. If floss shreds in the same gap twice, book a check-up and tell the dentist exactly which gap.

## How do you floss a genuinely tight contact?

If the tightness is even across your mouth and nothing is shredding, it is about technique and the right floss:

1. **Do not snap it through.** Forcing floss down in one go slams it into the gum and causes the cuts and soreness that make people give up.
2. **Use a gentle see-saw motion.** Hold the floss taut, ease it back and forth sideways, and let it slip past the contact point under its own weight.
3. **Make a C-shape.** Once through, wrap the floss around the side of one tooth and slide it just under the gum line, then repeat against the neighbouring tooth. The gap has two surfaces and each needs cleaning.
4. **Come out the same way you went in**, sideways rather than yanked upward.
5. **Switch product.** Waxed floss, PTFE floss and floss tape glide through tight contacts far more easily than unwaxed floss, which is the type most likely to shred.

If a contact is so tight that even waxed floss will not pass at all, do not keep forcing it. Mention it at your next visit. Sometimes it is simply how your teeth sit, and sometimes there is a reason worth finding.

## Should you use floss, an interdental brush, or a water flosser?

They are not interchangeable, and the right answer depends on the size of the gap. Most people need more than one.

- **Floss.** The right tool for tight contacts, where nothing else will fit. Cheap, effective, and it cleans the flat surfaces on both sides of the gap. It is also the tool people are worst at using consistently.
- **Interdental brushes.** Small bristled brushes that come in several sizes. Where there is enough space for one to fit without being forced, they generally clean better than floss and most people find them far easier to use. They are the tool of choice for larger gaps, gum recession, and cleaning under bridges. Forcing a brush that is too big into a tight gap damages the gum, so the size has to be matched to each space.
- **Water flossers.** A pressurised water jet that flushes debris and disrupts plaque along the gum line. They are genuinely useful for braces, bridges, implants, crowded teeth, and for anyone with arthritis or limited hand dexterity. They reduce bleeding and inflammation well. For very tight contacts, though, water does not replace the physical wipe of floss against the tooth surface, so treat it as a strong addition rather than a swap.

The practical answer for most mouths is interdental brushes wherever they fit, floss where they do not, and a water flosser on top if you have appliances or find the other two hard to manage. Your dentist or hygienist can tell you which sizes fit which of your gaps. That is a two-minute part of a check-up and it takes all the guesswork out.

## Do people use one floss pick for the entire mouth?

This is a fair question, and the honest answer is that most people do, and it is the weakest way to floss.

A pick holds a single short segment of floss stretched across a plastic fork. That same segment goes into every gap, so debris and plaque from one space get carried to the next, and the segment frays as it goes. With string floss you use a fresh section for each gap by winding it along your fingers as you work. That is the actual advantage of the string, and it is the reason dentists keep recommending it.

If picks are what you will realistically use every day, keep using them, because a pick used daily beats string floss used never. Just rinse the pick between teeth, replace it as soon as the floss frays or slackens, and consider using more than one per session. Better still, use interdental brushes for the gaps that allow them, since each brush is designed for repeated cleaning and rinsing.

## When should you clean between your teeth, and how often?

Once a day is the target for everyone. More than that has no added benefit and can irritate the gums. Time of day matters less than actually doing it, so pick the slot you will keep. There is reasonable evidence that cleaning between the teeth before brushing helps fluoride toothpaste reach the surfaces you have just opened up, so floss first and brush second if you have no strong habit already. Brushing alone leaves roughly a third of each tooth's surface untouched, which is why this step is not optional decoration on a routine.

## What if the gums bleed when you start?

Gums that have not been cleaned between for a long time often bleed for the first several days. If it settles within a week or two of daily cleaning, that was inflammation resolving. If bleeding continues beyond that, or happens in only one spot, it needs looking at rather than tolerating. There is a fuller explanation in our article on bleeding gums at celestialdental.org/bleeding-gums-causes-treatment-and-when-to-see-a-dentist. The one thing not to do is stop cleaning the area that bleeds.

## What about braces, bridges and implants?

Appliances create spaces that ordinary floss cannot reach on its own:

- **Braces.** Use a floss threader or superfloss to get under the archwire, or interdental brushes designed to fit around brackets. A water flosser makes daily cleaning realistic for most brace-wearers.
- **Bridges.** The underside of the false tooth has to be cleaned daily with superfloss or a threader, because nothing else reaches it.
- **Implants.** These need the same daily cleaning as natural teeth, using the brush size your dentist recommends, since gum inflammation around an implant is what threatens it long term.

## When should you see a dentist?

Book an appointment if floss shreds or catches in the same gap repeatedly, if food consistently packs into one space, if a gap has newly appeared between two teeth, if bleeding persists beyond two weeks of daily cleaning, or if you cannot get between your teeth at all and want to know why.
