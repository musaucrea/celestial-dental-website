If your teeth have chalky white flecks, cloudy patches or brown mottled bands that have been there since childhood, the likely cause is **dental fluorosis**. It is a change in the enamel that happens while adult teeth are still forming under the gum, in children exposed to more fluoride than the developing tooth can handle. It is not decay, it is not dirt, and it will not brush off. It is also common in Kenya, because groundwater in parts of the country, particularly the Rift Valley, is naturally high in fluoride.

The important thing to know before you spend money on it: **whitening alone often does not fix fluorosis**. Sometimes it makes the patches stand out more.

## How do you know it is fluorosis and not something else?

Fluorosis usually affects several teeth symmetrically, has been present since the teeth came through, and does not change much over time. Other causes look different:

- **Surface staining** from tea, coffee, cola, tobacco or chlorhexidine mouthwash. Usually yellow-brown, sitting on the outside of the enamel, and it comes off with a professional cleaning.
- **Decay.** Usually one spot on one tooth, often getting worse, sometimes with sensitivity or a visible hole.
- **One dark or grey tooth.** Often a tooth that has been knocked or root-canal treated, where the discolouration comes from inside the tooth.
- **Enamel hypoplasia or hypomineralisation.** Developmental defects that can look similar but have different causes, and that sometimes leave the enamel soft rather than just discoloured.

A dentist can usually separate these on examination, sometimes with an X-ray to check whether a dark tooth has a dead nerve. This matters because a single dark tooth may respond to internal bleaching, which does nothing for fluorosis, while fluorosis may need masking, which is overkill for a coffee stain.

## Why does whitening often disappoint on fluorosis?

Whitening gel lightens the whole tooth, including the healthy enamel around the patches. If the patch is white and opaque, lightening the background can reduce the contrast, which sometimes helps mild cases. If the patch is brown or deeply mottled, the surrounding enamel lightens faster than the stain does, and the mottling can look more obvious than before. Whitening also does not change texture, and moderate to severe fluorosis often has pitting you can feel with a fingernail.

Whitening still has a place. It is often the first and most conservative step in mild cases, or a preparation step before bonding so the shade of the new material matches a lighter tooth.

## What are the treatment options, from least to most invasive?

Think of it as a ladder. A good dentist starts at the bottom rung that will actually get you the result you want.

1. **Professional cleaning and polishing.** Removes surface stain sitting on top of the fluorosis, so you can see what you are actually dealing with.
2. **Whitening.** Lightens the background shade, and is most useful for mild, mainly-white fluorosis.
3. **Enamel microabrasion.** A controlled removal of a very thin surface layer, which can lift shallow brown staining. Often combined with whitening.
4. **Composite bonding, also called masking.** Tooth-coloured composite applied to the front surface to cover the discolouration. It is done in one visit, needs little or no tooth reduction, and can be repaired or reversed.
5. **Veneers.** Thin porcelain or composite shells over the front of the tooth, for widespread or severe discolouration where bonding will not hold up.
6. **Crowns.** Full coverage, generally reserved for teeth that are also broken, heavily filled or worn, rather than for discolouration alone.

## Can masking be done on all the teeth at once?

It can, and for even-looking results across a smile it often should be. Matching one masked tooth to five natural mottled ones is much harder than treating the whole visible zone together. But "all teeth" usually means the teeth that show when you smile, typically six to ten upper teeth and sometimes the lower front teeth. Molars at the back are rarely treated for appearance alone.

Composite bonding does have limits worth hearing before you commit. It can chip on a heavy bite, it picks up staining from tea, coffee and smoking over the years, and it usually needs polishing or repair periodically. That is a maintenance commitment rather than a one-off.

## Do veneers mean my teeth get shaved down?

It depends on the type. Conventional veneers need a thin layer of enamel removed so the veneer does not look bulky. That is irreversible, and the tooth will need a veneer or crown from then on. **Non-prep veneers** remove little or no enamel, but they only suit certain cases: teeth that are not already prominent, not crowded, and where you are covering colour rather than reshaping position.

Anyone offering veneers should tell you, before treatment, exactly how much enamel is coming off, whether the change is reversible, and what happens in ten years. If that conversation does not happen, ask for it.

## Does fluorosis weaken the teeth?

Mild fluorosis is a cosmetic issue and the enamel is generally sound. Moderate to severe fluorosis can involve pitted, more porous enamel that chips more easily and may be more sensitive. That is one reason a proper examination matters: the plan for a cosmetic-only case is different from a plan for enamel that also needs protecting.

## Can you prevent fluorosis in your children?

Fluorosis only develops while adult teeth are forming, roughly up to age eight. Once teeth are through, they cannot develop it. For young children:

- Know your water source. If your borehole or piped water is high in fluoride, ask your dentist about it and consider a filtered or alternative source for drinking and cooking.
- Use only a smear of toothpaste for under-threes and a pea-sized amount for older children.
- Supervise brushing so toothpaste is spat out rather than swallowed, and skip the fluoride mouthwash for young children.
- Do not add fluoride supplements unless a dentist has advised them for your child specifically.

## When should you see a dentist about stained teeth?

Book an appointment if a stain is new or getting worse, if only one tooth has changed colour, if there is sensitivity, a rough edge or a visible hole, or if the appearance is affecting your confidence enough that you are considering treatment. Do not use DIY whitening hacks such as baking soda scrubs, lemon juice, charcoal or unregulated bleaching kits on fluorosed enamel. They do not remove the patches and they can wear down the enamel you still have.
